SECTION 1

Instructor Specializations

Two instructors with complementary specializations cover the full engineering lifecycle. Both support all student projects; the split exists so students know who to go to for what.

Mr. Frank — Engineering Design & Fabrication (EDF)

Focus: turning ideas into physical things. The visual, structural, and manufacturable side of engineering.

  • Industrial and aesthetic design — user-centered design, form, ergonomics, visual impact
  • Technical graphics and documentation — CAD modeling, drafting standards including GD&T, architectural drawing, as-built documentation (Standard 2)
  • Prototyping and manufacturing — DFM for additive (3D printing) and subtractive (laser, CNC) processes (Standard 7)
  • Structural and construction principles — structural integrity, construction materials, civil engineering concepts (Standard 10)

EDP stages emphasized: Develop Solutions (form), Plan a Solution (physical build), Create (fabrication), Improve & Redesign, Present Your Solution.

Career paths: product design, industrial design, architectural engineering, design-focused mechanical engineering, manufacturing engineering, construction management, technical drafting.

Mr. Dryer — Engineering Systems & Emerging Concepts (ESEC)

Focus: the intelligence, functionality, and control that make engineered systems work. The digital, electrical, and analytical side.

  • Digital systems and controls — electrical fundamentals, digital electronics, logic design, Arduino/Python/PLC, control systems, mechatronics (Standards 5, 6, 9)
  • Software and algorithmic analysis — efficient code, software architecture, computational thinking (Standard 6)
  • Applied research and emerging technology — AI and ML in control systems, IoT, quantum computing concepts
  • System analysis and mechanical function — materials science, FEA theory, kinematics, dynamics, actuation (Standards 7, 8)

EDP stages emphasized: Identify the Problem (requirements), Research & Brainstorm, Develop Solutions (architecture, code), Create (functional prototypes), Test & Evaluate, Improve & Redesign.

Career paths: electrical engineering, software engineering, computer engineering, robotics, control systems, systems integration, R&D, automation engineering.

Teaching split

Mr. Frank teaches Grades 11 and 12; Mr. Dryer teaches Grades 9 and 10. The arrangement is collaborative — both teach across grades as needed, and all four years sit under the lead teacher's oversight.

SECTION 2

Foundational Pillars

Five pillars organize everything students do, from the daily Do Now to the capstone. Each answers a different question: what are you learning, what are you building, are you working safely, are you working professionally, and how do you know it worked.

Knowledge Consumption (KC)

Acquiring new information, concepts, and procedural skills — the "why" and the "how-to" before application.

Activities: Do Now! · Theory-Based Lesson · Training-Based Lesson
Roles: Research Engineer · Standards: 3, 5, 6, 7, 8, 10, 15

Applied Learning (AL)

Using what you've learned to solve problems, build designs, develop systems, and produce something real. The doing.

Activities: Project Work · Assignment · Lab & Testing · Independent Focus
Roles: Design, Development, Production & Construction Engineer · Standards: 3, 11, 12

Safety & Operations (S&O)

Keeping the shop secure, functional, and maintained. Equipment protocols, organization, stewardship.

Activities: Training-Based Lesson (safety) · Shop Stewardship
Roles: Production & Construction, Operations Engineer · Standard: 1

Professional Practice (PP)

The non-technical skills every engineering career needs — teamwork, communication, ethics, project management.

Activities: Professional Development Activity · One-on-One Mentoring
Roles: Management, Sales, Operations Engineer · Standards: 2, 4, 13, 14

Assessment & Reflection (A&R)

Evaluating outcomes, documenting progress, and thinking about your own thinking. Accountability and improvement.

Activities: Presentation/Review · Quiz/Test · Project Reflection · Engineering Logbook
Standards: 3, 11, 12, 13

The Engineering Logbook

The primary daily record. The day divides into three intervals — prior to break, break to lunch, lunch to end of day. Each carries an EDP status code and records work highlights, roadblocks, and captioned visual evidence. The day closes with challenges and troubleshooting, a daily synthesis, and next class's agenda.

EDP status codes

Five codes as working shorthand for the eight EDP stages. The compression is deliberate — a student choosing a code at the start of an interval shouldn't have to split hairs between adjacent planning stages.

Present Your Solution has no daily code — it is a project milestone rather than a daily working state.

Other documentation

"Do Now!" Reflection — title, equipment used, new knowledge acquired, brief process summary, application to current project, confidence self-rating.

Independent Focus Reflection — term-end self-assessment: project title, equipment, tasks completed, skills learned, status and progress, challenges and adaptations, project management evaluation, goals for next term.

Makerspace safety — the non-negotiables

  • Report all injuries immediately. If chemicals contact the eyes, wash for 15 minutes before seeking treatment.
  • Eye protection at all times. No loose clothing, ties, jewelry or gloves around moving machinery. Long hair tied back. Shoes covering the entire foot.
  • Never use a tool you haven't been trained on. Never work alone with power tools. Never walk away from a running tool.
  • Machines operate only with all required guards and shields in place.
  • Clean up every time you leave an area, including sweeping the floor. Shut off and unplug machines before cleaning or repair.

Cooperative Education is missing from this binder

The program of study names Grade 12 as "Engineering Technology IV / Cooperative Education" and makes Grade 11 students eligible from Term 3. Co-op appears nowhere else in the binder — no unit references it, no assessment accounts for it, and Grade 12's common assessments in the program of study include employer evaluations. A whole strand of the senior year is undocumented here.

Freshman Exploratory

Before the program proper: Grade 9 Terms 1 and 2 are exploratory. Two mini exploratory days, one per term, between them show every one of the school's eighteen shops; from each, a student picks four or five to spend a full vocational week in — nine week-long visits in all — then ranks their choices. Placement follows, and students join their shop for Terms 3 and 4.

The Engineering Technology exploratory covers the engineering design process, shop safety, hand sketching, CAD, 3D printing and professionalism — touching Standards 1, 2, 3, 7, 13, 14 and 15.

Universal Design for Learning

The program of study specifies UDL practices for every year: Engagement through hands-on labs and design challenges; Representation through visual models, videos and demonstrations; Action & Expression through oral explanations and skill demos.

Junior Capstone — a dual challenge

Unique Concept. An original concept with a distinctive structural or visual aspect — an upside-down building, a structure with integrated gardens. What makes it engineering rather than art is validating the concept's logic through research or testing.

Revitalization. Select an existing area, space or structure and plan how to bring new life to it — researching comparable efforts, developing a vision for how people would connect with the space, and pitching it.

Senior Capstone — 35 days

Not a guided assignment. Students identify their own problem, develop a solution, and manage the whole process. Project management — meticulous documentation, daily logbook, instructor meeting records. Research — define and justify a problem, evaluate approaches with a decision matrix. Design — concept to detailed design, analyzing viability, materials, production. Prototype, test, redesign — build, collect data, iterate. Reflection and presentation — defend the process and the critical decisions.
