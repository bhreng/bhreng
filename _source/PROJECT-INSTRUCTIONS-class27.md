## Term 1 Week 1 — Speaker Design

Design a custom casing for a Bluetooth speaker. The casing has to hold real
components, so this is a packaging problem as much as a styling one: the
speaker driver, the battery and the circuit board all have to fit, be held in
place, and go together in an order that works.

**What to do**

- Sketch on paper first. Explore shapes, forms and features, and develop at
  least two distinct concepts before you commit to one.
- Pick your strongest concept and model it in Fusion 360.
- Work out how each component is retained, and how the casing assembles. If it
  cannot be put together, it is not finished.
- Find the voltage your chosen speaker needs, and source a battery that matches
  it. Voltage is the only spec you need to match — ignore the rest.
- If time allows and the instructor approves your design, build a prototype.

**What you hand in**

- Two or more sketched concepts.
- A Fusion 360 model of the concept you chose.
- A parts list of the reference components: speaker, power connector, PCB.
- The speaker's voltage requirement and the battery you matched to it.
- A prototype, if you got that far.
- A short written reflection: what changed between your first sketch and the
  final model, and why.

**Watch out for**

Modelling the outside first and discovering the components do not fit. Place
the components as reference bodies before you shape anything around them.

## Term 1 Weeks 2–4 — Full Scope Project

Three weeks working as a different kind of engineer each week, producing what a
professional in that role would actually produce. The subject is a theme park
of your own design, so the roles have something concrete to act on.

**What to do**

- **Start with a vision statement.** Before you design anything, write a short
  statement covering the park's central theme or story; the experience you want
  visitors to have; the unique features that separate it from any other park;
  and the audience it is built for.
- Each week, choose a role — or more than one — from the table below and
  produce its deliverable. Seven roles across three weeks means some weeks
  carry two, so think about which pair well together.
- Every Monday, submit a Weekly Planner setting out your goals for the week.
- Every Friday, submit a Project Reflection along with all your project files.

**The roles and what each one produces**

| Role | Deliverable |
|---|---|
| Design Engineer | A detailed design: a drawing set or a CAD model. |
| Research Engineer | Research and a prediction: a new use for an existing item, or a new strategy for accomplishing a task. |
| Development Engineer | A model or a prototype. |
| Production & Construction Engineer | A Work Breakdown Structure, with a Gantt chart setting the sequence. |
| Operations Engineer | A large-scale system design, including operational notes. |
| Sales Engineer | A marketing piece — ad, commercial or brochure — that sells the engineering behind the product. |
| Management Engineer | An imaginary team, with tasks assigned to each person to complete a product. |

You can propose your own deliverable for a role instead, with approval.

**What you hand in**

- The vision statement.
- One deliverable per role, all seven covered by the end of week three.
- Three Weekly Planners and three Project Reflections.
- All project files.

## Term 1 Week 3 — Tiny House

The first large project of junior year. Design a detailed tiny house in Fusion
360. A tiny house is typically 400 square feet or less, so planning for a tight
space is the whole exercise. Think less about "rooms" and more about "spaces" —
how they blend into each other, and how one space can serve more than one
purpose.

**What to do**

- Start with brainstorming and conceptual design work.
- Write a design brief.
- Decide your approach: either convert an existing structure into a tiny house,
  or frame a design built from scratch.
- Model it in Fusion 360. Make every item its own component, and **name the
  components** — this will save you a great deal of time later.
- Work in multiple files if that suits you. Create a project folder and keep
  your parts as separate files inside it.
- Make furniture. You have the tables you built last year; include those. Using
  a good GrabCAD model is acceptable, but your own work has to be visible in
  the design too.
- Animate at least one item. A door counts, but a space-saving feature is a
  better answer.

**What you hand in**

- A design brief.
- The Fusion 360 3D model.
- 2D floor plans and elevations.
- 3D renderings.
- At least one animation.
- A Google Doc listing anything you borrowed, from a classmate or from online.

**Watch out for**

Modelling the whole house as one body. Separate, named components are what make
the drawings, the renders and the animation possible at the end.

## Term 2 Week 1 — ADU Design Project

The Massachusetts Affordable Homes Act lets homeowners in single-family zones
build one Accessory Dwelling Unit by right. That makes small, affordable
housing a real market. You are the design engineer for one such unit.

You will use the BHR Engineering Design Process and Documentation Procedures
throughout.

**What to do**

**Phase 1 — ideation and research.** Define the unit before you draw it:

- **Type** — detached new construction, garage conversion, or attached
  addition.
- **Target size** — the exact square footage of your floor plan, 900 sq ft or
  under.
- **Target user** — who lives here. An elderly parent, a college student,
  someone else.

Then sketch several conceptual floor plans — a studio layout, a one-bedroom, a
lofted design — and choose between them on three criteria: space utilisation,
estimated material cost (high, medium or low is enough), and compliance with
the Massachusetts ADU rules.

**Phase 2 — modelling and documentation.** Build the complete 3D model in
Fusion 360, showing the interior layout (kitchen, bath, sleeping area) and
every exterior component (walls, roof, windows, doors). Generate your 2D
drawings from that model rather than drawing them separately.

**Phase 3 — mid-project review.** Partway through, stop and check feasibility:
does the plan still meet the square footage limit, does the layout still work
for your target user, and is anything in the model going to be impossible to
build? Record what you changed as a result.

**Phase 4 — documentation and reflection.** Keep your Daily Journal Log running
the whole way: tasks completed, problems hit in Fusion 360, and how you solved
them.

**What you hand in**

- The ADU definition: type, exact square footage, target user.
- Your conceptual floor plan sketches, and a short justification of the one you
  chose against the three criteria.
- A complete Fusion 360 model.
- A **floor plan** drawing: dimensioned, with the kitchen, bath and separate
  entrance clearly labelled.
- An **elevation view** of one exterior side.
- The mid-project feasibility check.
- The completed Daily Journal Log.

**Watch out for**

The 900 sq ft ceiling is a hard constraint, not a target. Check your floor area
before you start detailing, not after.

## Term 2 Week 3 — VEX V5 Clawbot Project

Build the VEX V5 Clawbot, program it, and document the whole thing as one
professional project record. This project is assessed on Documentation,
Technical Knowledge and Analysis — three of the shop's core concepts — not on
getting a robot to move.

**What to do**

- Build the Clawbot using the provided build guides.
- Once it works, complete the lessons and activities on the VEX site under the
  Play, Apply, Rethink and Know sections.
- Document everything as you go, in one place.
- Test the finished robot, note where it is weak, and modify it in response.

**What you hand in**

One complete VEX Project Document in Google Slides, containing:

- **Log book** — the build process, the problems you hit in both building and
  programming, and the fixes you actually applied.
- **Major part list (BOM)** — a formal table of every major component (motors,
  sensors, brain), with port numbers and a concise function description for
  each.
- **Code and annotations** — screenshots of your VEX code, annotated to explain
  the goal and function of each block and variable.
- **Modification justification** — what you changed on the final build, each
  change tied to a specific weakness you observed during testing.

**Watch out for**

A modification you cannot justify. "It looked better" is not an answer; the
grade is in the link between the test result and the change.

## Term 2 Week 4 — Simple Machines to Functional Mechanisms

This project moves you from digital designer to mechanical engineer. You are
not drawing shapes; you are designing a machine that has to work in the real
world. The test is the physical gap: the tolerance that lets printed plastic
parts move against each other instead of fusing solid.

**What to do**

**Part 1 — the digital foundation.** Model and animate two different simple
machines of your choice: lever, wheel and axle, pulley, inclined plane, wedge,
screw. Each must have functional joints — revolute, slider, and so on.

**Part 2 — the compound prototype.** Research and design one mechanical fidget
or mechanism sandbox: a small handheld device using at least two simple
machines working together, that you can interact with by turning a crank,
pushing a lever or sliding a button. A coin vending fidget or a rack-and-pinion
slider is the kind of thing.

- Leave a tolerance gap between all moving parts. 0.3 mm is the suggested
  figure.
- Use a Motion Link so that moving the input part automatically moves the
  outputs.
- Print it, and make sure it moves freely.

**What you hand in**

- Three high-quality renders: one per simple machine, one for the mechanism.
- Animation video exports showing all three designs in motion.
- A functional 3D-printed version of the compound mechanism, moving freely
  without being forced.

**Watch out for**

If two parts touch each other in Fusion, they come off the printer permanently
fused. Check every interface for clearance before you send the file.

## Term 3 Week 2 — Creative Concept Design

One day of conceptual design. You have the floor, the tools, and total creative
freedom. Design, model and refine anything you can think of in a professional
CAD program — Fusion 360, Revit, AutoCAD.

**What to do**

  architectural families. This is not the place for Lego-style building.
- Pick something with enough depth to fill the day.

If the finished model looks like twenty minutes of work, it was. Aim past what
you are already comfortable with.

## Term 3 Weeks 3–4 — City Design

The whole class works as one firm to design, analyse and 3D print a modern
city. The class produces one digital master site plan and one physical scale
model. Everything has to fit together, which makes the shared standards as
important as the buildings.

**What to do**

**Phase 1 — the master plan, in Autodesk Forma.** The class acts as the urban
planning board.

- **Site selection** — choose a real-world location, or a fictional plot.
- **Environmental analysis** — use Forma's analysis tools for sun hours, wind
  and noise. That data decides where green space goes and where high-rises go.
- **Zoning** — divide the city into residential, commercial, industrial and
  transit zones. Each student or pair is assigned a zone.

**Phase 2 — the infrastructure standard.** The class agrees one connection
standard so that a building designed by one student fits the road designed by
another.

- **The grid** — every city block is an agreed size and shape.
- **The connection** — every tile uses the same system to connect on all four
  sides.
- **The road curb** — one agreed sidewalk height and road width, across the
  whole model.

**Phase 3 — architecture, in Fusion 360.** Once the grid is set, build your
blocks.

- **Massing** — use the Forma data to justify your design decisions. If a
  building needs a slanted roof because of the sun it gets, say so.
- **Modular design** — model directly onto a copy of the master base tile.
- **The featured building** — each student produces at least one building in
  high detail.

**Phase 4 — physical production.** Simple massing prints for background
buildings to save print time; high-detail prints for the featured buildings.
Prints are clicked into the master layout on the classroom table as they come
off.

**Project roles.** For the class to work as one team, these roles are assigned:

- **Project Manager** (1–2 students) — holds the Forma master file and keeps
  everyone to their deadlines.
- **Infrastructure Lead** — prints the road and park tiles that connect
  everyone else's work.
- **Zoning Leads** — one each for residential, commercial and industrial.

**What you hand in**

- Your zone, modelled on the master base tile.
- At least one high-detail featured building.
- Your printed tiles, fitting the class standard.
- A written reflection: what your role was, where the interface standard held
  and where it failed, and what you would set differently next time.

**Watch out for**

Modelling before the connection standard is agreed. Anything built to your own
dimensions will not fit the city, and it will have to be redone.

> Folds in *City Design - Mid-Project Design Review & Feasibility Check* and
> the reflection that was carried in the assignment title. This is the strongest
> project-management artefact in the course: a shared master file, an agreed
> interface standard, and named responsibility.

## Term 4 Week 5 — End-of-Year Vibecoding Team Challenge

Teams of four design, build and pitch an app, program or game, with an AI
writing the code. Your team owns the vision, the user experience, the logic and
the pitch; Gemini handles the syntax. Choose something you would actually use
or play.

**What to do**

**Assign the four roles.**

- **Product Owner** — directs the AI on the core vision, gameplay or utility
  functions, and owns the user experience.
- **Lead Systems Engineer** — takes the generated code and refines it.
- **Go-To-Market Specialist** — business strategy, marketing angles, financial
  feasibility.
- **Creative Director** — visual design, presentation flow, and asset requests.

**Days 1–2: vision and business strategy.** Before you write anything, use
Gemini to establish the product's identity and draft a one-page business plan:
target audience, unique features, marketing strategy.

**Days 3–4: build the prototype.** Whether it is Python, HTML/JavaScript or a
custom script, have the AI generate code while you assemble it, test it and
direct the changes. When something breaks, paste the error back in and say
where it broke.

**Day 5: the pitch.** Combine the business plan and the working demo into one
presentation for the class.

**What you hand in**

One Product Launch Package per team:

- **Functional prototype** — the actual script, program link, or code
  repository. It needs to work at a basic level; it does not need to be
  polished.
- **One-page business summary** — AI-drafted, student-edited, covering why the
  product matters.
- **Pitch slide deck**.

**Watch out for**

Accepting generated code you cannot explain. You will be asked what a section
does during the pitch.

## End of year — Grade 11 Capstone

The capstone runs across four weeks and asks you to demonstrate what you
understand about civil engineering and architecture by managing two distinct
projects at the same time. One challenges your ability to prove engineering
logic; the other challenges your ability to sell a professional vision.

**What to do**

**Project 1 — a unique concept.** Develop an original structure that stretches
the boundaries of traditional design: an upside-down building, an integrated
vertical ecosystem, something equally unreasonable. Then prove it is physically
and logically viable. Look at your own design through an engineer's eyes and
justify why it works.

**Project 2 — the professional vision.** Present a design as a firm would
present it to a client: the concept, the reasoning, and the case for building
it.

**The four weeks.** Week 1 is conceptual design. Week 2 is detailed design.
Weeks 3 and 4 are proof — research, analysis and the presentation materials.

**What you hand in**

- **Engineering Logic Report** — documented research on comparable structures,
  or the scientific principles that support your concept.
- **Structural Analysis** — simulation and test results, from Fusion 360 or
  equivalent tools.
- The presentation package for Project 2.
- A weekly journal across all four weeks.

**Watch out for**

Running both projects sequentially instead of in parallel. Week 4 is not enough
time to start the second one.

## Running all year — Independent Study Project

A year-long project in whatever part of engineering actually interests you.
This is the one you run at your own pace, treated as a personal engineering
hobby. It breaks into three areas, which can all serve one larger idea or stay
completely separate.

**What to do**

- **Research** — choose a concept, theory or process you are curious about.
  Conduct a deep dive using reputable sources, and end at its real-world
  applications.
- **Design** — design a new product or solution. It can stand alone or be a
  component of something larger: an ergonomic desk organiser, a custom phone
  case.
- **Development** — take something that already exists and fix, modify or
  improve it. This one is about proficiency with the software and tools.
- Keep a weekly journal throughout: research findings, what you learned, what
  went wrong. Sketches, notes and video logs all count.

**What you hand in**

- The three project outputs, or documented progress on them.
- The weekly journal.
- A mid-year reflection and an end-of-year reflection on each project.

**Watch out for**

If you do not have a project idea yet, do not wait for one. Build a skill tree
in these three areas so you are ready when an idea does arrive — that is what
the seven pathway guides are for.
