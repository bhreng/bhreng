# Section 7 — Curriculum Standards Analysis

## 1.0 Program Framework and Analytical Context

This report provides a detailed analysis of the BHR Engineering Technology program's curriculum, specifically mapping State Standards 3 through 10 to its new pedagogical architecture. This analysis serves two primary functions: first, to validate the programmatic coherence of the curriculum by demonstrating a logical and progressive sequence of skill acquisition; and second, to document how the program’s dual-axis framework of instructional specializations and pedagogical pillars ensures comprehensive competency development for all students.

### 1.1 Dual-Specialization Instructional Model

The program is built upon the complementary expertise of two lead instructors, each heading a distinct specialization. This model provides students with targeted, expert mentorship across the full engineering lifecycle.

### 1.2 The Five Foundational Pillars of Instruction

The five foundational pillars represent the "how" of learning within the program. They are the pedagogical framework guiding all student activities to ensure a holistic educational experience that integrates theory, hands-on application, safety, professional skills, and reflective evaluation.

  - Knowledge Consumption (KC): This pillar focuses on the acquisition of new information, concepts, and foundational procedural skills.
  - Applied Learning (AL): This pillar is where students actively implement acquired knowledge and skills to solve problems, create designs, develop systems, and produce tangible outcomes.
  - Safety & Operations (S&O): This pillar instills the critical importance of ensuring a secure, functional, and well-maintained learning and working environment.
  - Professional Practice (PP): This pillar develops the non-technical, "soft skills" and professional attributes essential for success in any engineering career.
  - Assessment & Reflection (A&R): This pillar focuses on evaluating learning outcomes, documenting progress, and fostering metacognition—the ability to think about one's own learning and problem-solving processes.

### 1.3 The Ten Program Content Categories (PCCs)

The ten Program Content Categories (PCCs) represent the "what" of the curriculum—the specific knowledge domains and skill sets that students will master. These categories are strategically aligned with the instructor specializations to ensure expert-led instruction.

This framework of specializations, pillars, and content categories provides the necessary context for the granular, standard-by-standard analysis that follows.

## 3.0 Analysis of Progressive Rigor

This analysis examines the evolution of skills and complexity across the four-year program, leveraging the data from the standards map. It demonstrates how foundational skills introduced in early standards serve as the necessary building blocks for the high-level application and synthesis required in upper-level capstone projects, validating the curriculum's intentional design for progressive rigor.

### 3.1 From Fundamentals to Application

Skills from Standard 3: Fundamentals of Engineering are predominantly introduced in Grade 9, establishing the core competencies required for all future work. In **Unit 2: Intro to Digital Design & CAD**, students are introduced to basic parametric modeling (S3-i) and creating digital sketches (S3-h). Concurrently, in **Unit 3: Engineering Design & Planning**, they learn the foundational, non-technical processes of engineering, such as brainstorming (S3-c), problem identification (S3-a), and establishing project constraints (S3-j). These skills represent the introductory, foundational competencies upon which all subsequent technical and project-based learning is built.

### 3.2 Culmination in Capstone Projects

In stark contrast to the introductory skills of Grade 9, the capstone projects of Grades 11 and 12 demand a sophisticated synthesis of advanced competencies across multiple standards. The progression from simple ideation to complex execution is stark; for example, the Grade 9 introduction to team brainstorming (S3-c) evolves into the professional-level risk assessment and mitigation (S4-e) required of seniors.

The Grade 11 **Junior Capstone**, focused on Standard 10: Civil Engineering, Architecture Demonstration, Design, and Implementation, requires students to undertake two distinct challenges: a **"Unique Concept"** project and a **"Revitalization"** project. Here, students must move beyond basic modeling (S3-h) to engage in complex tasks such as performing structural analysis with specialized software (S10-h), applying principles of sustainable design (S10-v), and producing entire construction document sets complete with plans, sections, and schedules (S10-b). This requires the integration of technical skill, aesthetic judgment, and regulatory awareness.

The Grade 12 **Senior Capstone** elevates this integration to a professional level, drawing heavily on advanced skills from Standards 3 and 4. Students must manage a **35-day, self-directed project** that requires not just fabrication but comprehensive project management and a formal defense. This includes not only advanced fabrication (S4-h) but also professional-level diagnostics (S3-m) and the implementation of a continuous improvement process through iterative redesign (S3-q).

This clear progression from discrete, foundational skills to integrated, mastery-level execution validates a curriculum designed to move students from introductory knowledge to professional application, a rigorous process whose evidence is meticulously captured through assessment tools designed to foster student reflection.

## 4.0 Documentation and Accountability for Assessment & Reflection (A&R)

The Assessment & Reflection (A&R) pillar is of paramount strategic importance to the program, serving as the primary mechanism for documenting progress, fostering metacognition, and providing tangible evidence of learning. This section details the specific tools used to fulfill the A&R pillar's objectives, ensuring that students are not only completing work but are actively thinking about their own learning and problem-solving processes.

  - **Daily Logbook:** This serves as the primary tool for documenting day-to-day work. Students use the logbook to record the specific project they are working on, list the tools and equipment used, and detail their accomplishments and any challenges they encountered. It is the core instrument for tracking incremental progress.
  - **"Do Now!" Reflection:** Following short, skill-based "Do Now!" activities, this template functions as a quick self-assessment tool. It solidifies newly acquired micro-skills by prompting students to identify the skill learned, briefly summarize the process they followed, and consider how the new competency can be immediately applied to their current projects.
  - **Independent Focus Reflection:** This formal, term-end self-assessment requires students to evaluate their progress on their self-directed Independent Focus work, against the proposal they submitted at the start of the term. It is a structured opportunity to analyze challenges, reflect on the effectiveness of their project management strategies, and set actionable goals for the following term.

These documentation practices are the primary mechanisms for capturing evidence of the A&R pillar's application across all standards and projects. This detailed tracking provides a clear line of sight into the program's overall effectiveness, creating a foundation for the high-level findings presented in the executive summary.

## 5.0 Executive Summary for Program Administration

This analysis confirms that the BHR Engineering Technology program's curriculum is coherent, rigorous, and systematically designed to build student competency. The detailed mapping of State Standards 3 through 10 demonstrates a clear and intentional progression, moving students from foundational skills in Grade 9 to advanced, integrated application in the junior and senior capstone projects.

This structured approach ensures that the program meets the rigorous requirements of the state frameworks. The curriculum's alignment with the five foundational pillars and ten content categories provides a robust architecture for delivering comprehensive, industry-relevant instruction. This alignment directly prepares students to achieve industry-recognized credentials, specifically the **OSHA 10** and **Autodesk Certified User (ACU)** in multiple platforms (the shop is Autodesk-only; the framework's SolidWorks credential is not pursued), thereby validating the program's effectiveness in preparing students for success in post-secondary education and professional careers.
