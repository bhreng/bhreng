BHR Engineering · Independent Focus

Pick your pathway

Once you know your way around the shop, you get to choose a direction and take it further than the regular class has time for. Seven to choose from. This is how you decide.

What a pathway actually is

A pathway isn't another class. It's a place to work on something you chose, for a term, with the tools and reading already gathered for you.

You still have your regular engineering class — that's where you're graded and where the whole group moves together. The pathway is where you take one thing further. You pick the project, you set the pace, and a teacher checks in rather than assigns.

Each pathway has five sections: Explore the Field, Learn the Concepts, Build Your Skills, Find a Project, and Get the Files. Same five in all seven, so once you know your way around one, you know your way around all of them.

Three questions worth asking yourself

What do you want to be holding at the end?

A physical object you built? A machine that moves on its own? A drawing set someone could build from? Working code? Start from the finished thing and work backward.

What part do you actually enjoy?

Some people like the figuring-out. Some like the making. Some like it when something finally works. All three are engineering — they just point at different pathways.

What would you want to try before you have to commit?

You're not signing up for a career. Picking a pathway you end up not loving still teaches you something worth knowing about yourself.

Who helps you with what

Mr. Frank

Helps you make it real — how it looks, how it's drawn, how it gets built.

  • Designing something in CAD that actually looks good
  • Getting a part ready to 3D print or laser cut
  • Making a structure that holds up
  • Drawings someone else could build from
  • Fixing a prototype that didn't come out right

Mr. Dryer

Helps you make it work — circuits, code, and proving it performs.

  • Designing or troubleshooting a circuit
  • Writing and debugging code for a microcontroller or robot
  • Figuring out why a system isn't behaving
  • Getting sensors and motors working together
  • Using data or simulation to show it works

Once you've picked

  1. Start in Explore the Field

    Read enough to know what people in this field actually do all day. You're checking whether you still want it once you know more.

  2. Pick something to build

    Go to Find a Project and take one, or bring your own idea and talk it through with your teacher. Your own idea is usually better, because you'll care more when it stops working.

  3. Learn what that project needs

    Not everything — just what this build requires. Learn the Concepts for the theory, Build Your Skills for the tools. You'll come back for more as you hit things you can't do yet.

  4. Keep your logbook as you go

    Every day, in the moment — not from memory at the end of the week. It's how you show what you did and how you get credit for the parts nobody saw.

  5. Show your work

    At the end of the term you explain what you set out to do, what happened, and what you'd do differently. Things going wrong isn't a problem here. Not being able to explain why is.

Before you start

You need your OSHA 10 safety credential before doing shop work in any pathway. It's the same requirement across all seven, and it isn't a formality — it's the reason you're allowed near the equipment.
