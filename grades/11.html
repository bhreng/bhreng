Speaker Design

Wk 1 Project

A custom casing for a Bluetooth speaker, designed around the components that have to fit inside it.

The first project of the year, and it is a warm-up on purpose — it puts the whole design process back in your hands after the summer.

It is not just a nice-looking box. You are designing for the components: how the speaker, the battery and the circuit board are held, and how the whole thing goes together.

What you hand in

  • At least two distinct concepts, sketched on paper first
  • A Fusion 360 model of the concept you chose
  • A parts list of the reference components — speakers, power connectors, PCB
  • The voltage requirement of your speaker, and a battery that matches it
  • A prototype, if there is time and the design is approved
HideRead the full brief

Welcome back to the shop! To get our brains and our design skills warmed up for the year, we're going to dive into a project that combines creativity with engineering principles: designing a custom casing for a Bluetooth speaker. This is a chance to shake off the summer rust and get familiar with our design process and tools again.

Your goal is to design a unique and functional casing for a Bluetooth speaker. This isn't just about making a cool-looking box; you'll need to design for the components, considering how everything fits together and how the final product will be assembled. While we'll have a separate lesson on the internal speaker components, your design should be ready to house them. Think about how the speakers, battery, and circuit board will be secured inside your design.

Deliverables

  • Initial Sketches — Begin by brainstorming and sketching your ideas on paper. Think about different shapes, forms, and features. Don't worry about perfection; focus on exploring a variety of concepts. You should aim to have at least two distinct design concepts.
  • Fusion 360 Model — Once you've chosen your best concept, you will translate your 2D sketches into a detailed 3D model in Fusion 360.
  • Parts list — include a list of reference components (i.e., speakers, power supply connectors, and PCB).
  • Power Requirements — Find the voltage requirement of the speaker you chose and source an applicable battery (only look for the appropriate voltage when looking for the battery, don't worry about anything else).
  • Prototype — If time allows create a prototype from an approved (by instructor) design.

Companions in the same topic: BHR Engineering Shop Safety Test, BHR Engineering Shop Safety Acknowledgement Form, Speaker Project Reflection.

The safety test and acknowledgement are in this same week. Nothing gets built before they are done.

ADU Design Project

Wk 1 Project

A 3D model of an Accessory Dwelling Unit, under the Massachusetts Affordable Homes Act.

Massachusetts now lets homeowners in single-family zones build one ADU by right. That is a real market for small, affordable housing, and you are the design engineer for one.

Phase 1 — ideation and research. Define your ADU, then sketch several conceptual floor plans (studio, one-bedroom, lofted) and pick one on space use, rough material cost, and MA ADU compliance.

Phase 2 — modelling and documentation. Build the full model, then generate the drawings from it.

Phase 3 — reflection. Keep the Daily Journal Log running the whole way, including what went wrong in Fusion and how you got out of it.

What you hand in

  • ADU type — detached new build, garage conversion, or attached addition
  • Target size — the exact square footage, which must be 900 sq ft or under
  • Target user — who actually lives in it
  • A complete Fusion 360 model showing kitchen, bath, sleeping area, walls, roof, windows and doors
  • A dimensioned floor plan, labelled, with the separate entrance shown
  • An elevation view of one exterior side
HideRead the full brief

The Massachusetts Affordable Homes Act now allows homeowners in single-family zones to build one Accessory Dwelling Unit (ADU) "by-right," which simplifies the process and creates a massive market opportunity for small-scale, affordable housing.

Your challenge is to act as a Design Engineer and create a 3D model for a new ADU.

Project Deliverables & BHR Documentation. You will use the BHR Engineering Design Process and Documentation Procedures to complete this project.

Phase 1: Ideation & Research (Project Start / Design Brief)

Project / Design Prep — Define your ADU:

  • ADU Type — Specify the type you are designing (e.g. Detached New Construction (freestanding), Garage Conversion, or Attached Addition).
  • Target Size — State the exact square footage of your floor plan (must be ≤ 900 sq ft).
  • Target User — Who is this unit for? (e.g. an elderly parent, a college student.)

Then: start by creating different conceptual floor plan sketches (e.g. studio layout, one-bedroom layout, lofted design). Evaluate and select the best concept based on Space Utilization, Estimated Material Cost (simple high/medium/low), and MA ADU Compliance.

Phase 2: Modeling and Documentation (Detail Design Work)

  • Fusion 360 3D Model — Develop a complete 3D model of your selected ADU design. This model must clearly show the interior layout (kitchen, bath, sleeping area) and all exterior components (walls, roof, windows, doors).
  • Technical Graphics Output — From your 3D model, generate at least two professional 2D drawings: a Floor Plan (dimensions and clear labelling for the kitchen, bath, and separate entrance) and an Elevation View (the exterior from one side).

Phase 3: Project Reflection

  • Daily Documentation — Maintain your Daily Journal Log throughout the project, documenting tasks completed, any issues encountered with Fusion 360, and how you solved them.

Simple Machines to Functional Mechanisms

Wk 4 Project

A handheld mechanism that comes off the printer already moving.

You move from digital designer to mechanical engineer here. The real test is the physical gap: the tolerance that lets printed plastic parts move against each other instead of fusing solid.

Part 1 — the digital foundation. Model and animate two different simple machines of your choice, each with working joints (revolute, slider, and so on).

Part 2 — the mechanism. Design one mechanical fidget or mechanism sandbox: something you hold and interact with, using at least two simple machines together. A coin vending fidget or a rack-and-pinion slider are the kind of thing.

What you hand in

  • Three renders — one per simple machine, one for the mechanism
  • Animation exports showing all three in motion
  • A motion link, so moving the input moves the outputs
  • A printed prototype that moves freely without being forced
HideRead the full brief

In this project, you will move from "Digital Designer" to "Mechanical Engineer." You aren't just drawing shapes; you are designing a machine that must work in the real world. You will master Joints and Motion Links in Fusion 360 and then bring your most complex design to life using our 3D printers.

Your mission is to prove that you can account for the "Physical Gap" — the tolerances required to make plastic parts move against each other without fusing together.

Part 1: The Digital Foundation (2 Simple Machines). Before building your final 3D printed design, you must demonstrate mastery of the basics. Model and animate two different simple machines of your choice (e.g. lever, wheel and axle, pulley, inclined plane, wedge, screw). Requirement: each must have functional Joints (Revolute, Slider, etc). Deliverable: a high-quality render and a short animation of each.

Part 2: The "Mechanism Thing" (Compound Prototype). Research and design one "Mechanical Fidget" or "Mechanism Sandbox." This is a small, handheld device that uses at least two simple machines working together.

  • Inspiration: Think of the "Coin Vending Fidget" or a "Rack and Pinion Slider." It should be something you can hold in your hand and interact with (turn a crank, push a lever, slide a button).
  • The Constraint: You must use a gap for tolerance (suggestion of 0.3 mm) between all moving parts. If your parts touch in Fusion, they will be stuck forever in the 3D printer!
  • The "Link": Use a Motion Link to show how moving one part (the input) automatically moves the others (the outputs).

Submission Checklist

  • The Digital (Fusion 360) — Renders: 3 total (one for each simple machine, one for the complex mechanism). Animations: video exports showing all 3 designs in motion.
  • The Physical Prototype — A functional, 3D-printed version of your complex mechanism. It must move freely without being forced or broken.

Leave a tolerance gap between all moving parts — 0.3 mm is the suggested figure. If the parts touch in Fusion, they come off the printer stuck together permanently.

City Design

Wk 3–4 Project

The whole class as one firm, designing and printing a modern city that has to fit together.

Phase 1 — the master plan. The class acts as the urban planning board. Pick a real or fictional site in Autodesk Forma, run its analysis for sun hours, wind and noise, and let that data decide where green space goes and where the high-rises go. Then zone the city and assign each student a zone.

Phase 2 — the infrastructure standard. The class agrees one connection standard so a building designed by one student fits the road designed by another: block size, the four-sided connection system, and the exact sidewalk height and road width.

Phase 3 — architecture. Each student models onto a copy of the master base tile, using the Forma data to justify the design, and builds at least one high-detail featured building.

Phase 4 — production. Simple massing prints for the background, high detail for the featured buildings, all clicked into the master layout on the classroom table.

What you hand in

  • One digital master site plan
  • One physical scale model
  • Named roles: Project Manager holds the Forma master file and the deadlines; Infrastructure Lead prints the road and park tiles everyone connects to; Zoning Leads for residential, commercial and industrial
HideRead the full brief

Objective: Work as a unified firm to design, analyze, and 3D print a modern city. The class will produce one digital "Master Site Plan" and one physical "Scale Model."

Phase 1: The Master Plan (Autodesk Forma). The class will act as the Urban Planning Board. Before anyone models a building, we must define the world.

  • Site Selection — Use Autodesk Forma to select a real-world location (or a fictional plot).
  • Environmental Analysis — Use Forma's AI tools to check sun hours, wind patterns, and noise. This data should dictate where "Green Space" goes and where "High-Rises" go.
  • Zoning — Divide the city into zones (Residential, Commercial, Industrial, Transit). Each student/pair will be assigned a specific zone.

Phase 2: The Infrastructure Standard (The "Base Tile"). To make the city model modular, the whole class must agree on a Connection Standard. This ensures that a building designed by Student A fits perfectly next to a road designed by Student B.

  • The Grid — Every city block will be an agreed upon size and shape.
  • The Connection — Each tile must feature a predetermined system to connect all four sides.
  • The "Road Curb" — You must agree on the height of the sidewalk and the width of the road so they line up across the whole model.

Phase 3: Architecture & Modeling (Fusion 360). Once the grid is set, students move into Fusion 360 to build their specific blocks.

  • Building Massing — Use the data from Forma to design buildings that make sense for the environment (e.g. "This building needs a slanted roof because it gets the most sun").
  • Modular Design — Students model their building directly onto a copy of the Master Base Tile.
  • The "Featured" Building — Each student must have at least one "Featured" building designed in high detail.

Phase 4: Physical Production (3D Printing).

  • Basic Buildings — Use "Massing" models (simple geometric shapes) for the background buildings to save print time.
  • Featured Buildings — High-detail prints for the centrepieces of the model.
  • The Assembly — As prints finish, they are "clicked" into the master city layout on a large table in the classroom.

New Project Roles (Industry Simulation). To make the "One Big Team" work, assign students specific leadership roles:

  • Project Manager (1–2 students) — Manages the Forma Master File and ensures everyone is meeting their deadlines.
  • Infrastructure Lead — Responsible for printing the "Road" and "Park" tiles that connect everyone else's buildings.
  • Zoning Leads — One lead for Residential, Commercial, and Industrial.

Welcome to your final engineering challenge of the year! This week, we are throwing out the stress of manual syntax writing and stepping into the shoes of Tech Directors and Creative Founders.

Your mission is to work in small teams to design, code, and pitch a brand-new app, program, or game using "Vibecoding" — letting Google Gemini do the heavy lifting of writing the actual code while your team focuses on the vision, user experience, logic, and presentation. This project is meant to be fast, collaborative, and, most importantly, fun. Choose something you actually want to play or use!

The Team Roles. Every team needs a cross-functional squad to handle the launch. Divide these roles among your team:

  • The Product Owner (The Vibe Director) — Directs the AI on the core vision, gameplay, or utility functions. Manages the ultimate user experience.
  • The Lead Systems Engineer (The Code Prompter) — Takes and refines the program code.
  • The Go-To-Market Specialist (The Business Lead) — Uses Gemini to generate the business strategy, marketing angles, and financial feasibility.
  • The Creative Director (The Pitch Deck Designer) — Leads the visual aesthetics, presentation flow, and asset requests.

The 5-Day Sprint Roadmap

Days 1–2: Vision & Business Strategy. Before you code, you need a plan. Use Gemini to establish your product's identity. Power prompt: "We are a team of students building a casual [mobile game / productivity app / web tool] called [Name]. You are our business consultant. Open a Canvas and help us draft a 1-page business plan, including our target audience, unique features, and a fun marketing strategy."

Days 3–4: The Vibecoding Hackathon. Time to build. Use Gemini to write the prototype. Whether it's Python, HTML/JavaScript, or a custom script, let the AI generate the code files while you piece them together, test them, and direct changes. Power prompt: "You are our Lead software engineer. We want to write a functional prototype of a game where [explain your simple game mechanics, e.g. a ball dodging falling spikes]. Provide the clean, commented [HTML/JS or Python] code block for this setup so we can run it." Note: if the code breaks, just paste the error back into Gemini and say: "This broke at line 14, debug it for me!"

Day 5: The Final Pitch Showcase. Synthesize your business plan and code demo into a light, punchy presentation deck for the class.

Required Team Deliverables. Your team will submit a single Product Launch Package containing: the Functional Prototype (the actual script, program link, or code repository generated through your vibecoding sessions — it just needs to work at a basic level), the 1-Page Business Summary (an AI-synthesized, student-polished plan covering why your app matters), and the Pitch Slide Deck.

How to Start. Get into your teams, open up Gemini, and copy/paste this exact prompt to kickstart the brainstorming engine: "Gemini, our team has 4 days to build a fun, light, end-of-year software prototype. Give us 3 unique, engaging, and highly achievable ideas for a web-based game or unique utility app that students our age would find cool. For each idea, briefly list what programming language we should use."

All year Project

A year-long project in whatever part of engineering actually interests you.

This is the one you run at your own pace, treated like a personal engineering hobby. It breaks into three areas, which can all serve one big idea or stay completely separate.

What you hand in

HideRead the full brief

This is a year-long, independent project where you will explore an area of engineering that truly interests you. This is your chance to experiment and develop valuable skills at your own pace, treating your work like a personal engineering hobby.

Project Breakdown: Research, Design, and Development. Independent study will be broken down into three different project areas: research, design, and development. You can choose to have all three projects connected to one overarching idea, or you can have them be separate and distinct from one another. If you're struggling to come up with a specific project, you can focus on building up a "skill tree" in these areas to prepare you for when a project idea strikes.

  • Research Project — Choose a specific engineering concept, theory, or process that sparks your curiosity. Your goal is to conduct a deep dive into the topic, gathering information from reputable sources and exploring its real-world applications.
  • Design Project — This can be a stand-alone design or a component of a larger project. You will focus on designing a new product or solution, such as an ergonomic desk organizer or a custom phone case. This is where you'll cultivate your design thinking and problem-solving skills.
  • Development Project — If you have an existing product or idea, you can choose to fix, modify, or improve it. This project is about gaining proficiency in relevant software and tools and applying your knowledge to a functional product.

Documentation and Reflection. To keep track of your progress, you'll be responsible for submitting reflections at the mid-point and end of the year for each of your projects. You will also maintain a weekly journal to document your research findings, learnings, and any challenges you encounter. This can include sketches, notes, or even video logs.

If you have no idea yet, build a skill tree in these areas so you are ready when an idea does strike. That is exactly what the seven pathway guides are for.
