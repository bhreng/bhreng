Speaker Design

Wk 1 Project

A custom casing for a Bluetooth speaker, designed around the components that have to fit inside it.

The first project of the year, and it is a warm-up on purpose — it puts the whole design process back in your hands after the summer.

It is not just a nice-looking box. You are designing for the components: how the speaker, the battery and the circuit board are held, and how the whole thing goes together.

What you hand in

  • At least two distinct concepts, sketched on paper first
  • A Fusion 360 model of the concept you chose
  • A parts list of the reference components — speakers, power connectors, PCB
  • The voltage requirement of your speaker, and a battery that matches it
  • A prototype, if there is time and the design is approved
  • A short reflection: what changed between your first sketch and the final model, and why
HideRead the full brief

Design a custom casing for a Bluetooth speaker. The casing has to hold real components, so this is a packaging problem as much as a styling one: the speaker driver, the battery and the circuit board all have to fit, be held in place, and go together in an order that works.

What to do

  • Sketch on paper first. Explore shapes, forms and features, and develop at least two distinct concepts before you commit to one.
  • Pick your strongest concept and model it in Fusion 360.
  • Work out how each component is retained, and how the casing assembles. If it cannot be put together, it is not finished.
  • Find the voltage your chosen speaker needs, and source a battery that matches it. Voltage is the only spec you need to match — ignore the rest.
  • If time allows and the instructor approves your design, build a prototype.

What you hand in

  • Two or more sketched concepts.
  • A Fusion 360 model of the concept you chose.
  • A parts list of the reference components: speaker, power connector, PCB.
  • The speaker's voltage requirement and the battery you matched to it.
  • A prototype, if you got that far.
  • A short written reflection: what changed between your first sketch and the final model, and why.

Watch out for

Modelling the outside first and discovering the components do not fit. Place the components as reference bodies before you shape anything around them.

The safety test and acknowledgement are in this same week. Nothing gets built before they are done.

Tiny House

The first large project of junior year. Think less about “rooms” and more about “spaces”: how they blend into each other, and how one space can serve more than one purpose.

You can either convert an existing structure into a tiny house, or frame a design built from scratch.

What you hand in

  • A design brief
  • The Fusion 360 model, every item its own named component
  • 2D floor plans and elevations
  • 3D renderings
  • At least one animated item — a door counts, but a space-saving feature is a better answer
  • A Google Doc listing anything you borrowed, from a classmate or from online
HideRead the full brief

The first large project of junior year. Design a detailed tiny house in Fusion 360. A tiny house is typically 400 square feet or less, so planning for a tight space is the whole exercise. Think less about "rooms" and more about "spaces" — how they blend into each other, and how one space can serve more than one purpose.

What to do

  • Start with brainstorming and conceptual design work.
  • Write a design brief.
  • Decide your approach: either convert an existing structure into a tiny house, or frame a design built from scratch.
  • Model it in Fusion 360. Make every item its own component, and name the components — this will save you a great deal of time later.
  • Work in multiple files if that suits you. Create a project folder and keep your parts as separate files inside it.
  • Make furniture. You have the tables you built last year; include those. Using a good GrabCAD model is acceptable, but your own work has to be visible in the design too.
  • Animate at least one item. A door counts, but a space-saving feature is a better answer.

What you hand in

  • A design brief.
  • The Fusion 360 3D model.
  • 2D floor plans and elevations.
  • 3D renderings.
  • At least one animation.
  • A Google Doc listing anything you borrowed, from a classmate or from online.

Watch out for

Modelling the whole house as one body. Separate, named components are what make the drawings, the renders and the animation possible at the end.

Work in multiple files if it suits you — a project folder with each part as its own file. Include the tables you built last year, and make some of the furniture yourself rather than pulling all of it from GrabCAD.

ADU Design Project

Wk 1 Project

A 3D model of an Accessory Dwelling Unit, under the Massachusetts Affordable Homes Act.

Massachusetts now lets homeowners in single-family zones build one ADU by right. That is a real market for small, affordable housing, and you are the design engineer for one.

Phase 1 — ideation and research. Define your ADU, then sketch several conceptual floor plans (studio, one-bedroom, lofted) and pick one on space use, rough material cost, and MA ADU compliance.

Phase 2 — modelling and documentation. Build the full model, then generate the drawings from it.

Phase 3 — reflection. Keep the Daily Journal Log running the whole way, including what went wrong in Fusion and how you got out of it.

What you hand in

  • ADU type — detached new build, garage conversion, or attached addition
  • Target size — the exact square footage, which must be 900 sq ft or under
  • Target user — who actually lives in it
  • A complete Fusion 360 model showing kitchen, bath, sleeping area, walls, roof, windows and doors
  • A dimensioned floor plan, labelled, with the separate entrance shown
  • An elevation view of one exterior side
  • The mid-project feasibility check
  • The completed Daily Journal Log
HideRead the full brief

The Massachusetts Affordable Homes Act lets homeowners in single-family zones build one Accessory Dwelling Unit by right. That makes small, affordable housing a real market. You are the design engineer for one such unit.

You will use the BHR Engineering Design Process and Documentation Procedures throughout.

What to do

Phase 1 — ideation and research. Define the unit before you draw it:

  • Type — detached new construction, garage conversion, or attached addition.
  • Target size — the exact square footage of your floor plan, 900 sq ft or under.
  • Target user — who lives here. An elderly parent, a college student, someone else.

Then sketch several conceptual floor plans — a studio layout, a one-bedroom, a lofted design — and choose between them on three criteria: space utilisation, estimated material cost (high, medium or low is enough), and compliance with the Massachusetts ADU rules.

Phase 2 — modelling and documentation. Build the complete 3D model in Fusion 360, showing the interior layout (kitchen, bath, sleeping area) and every exterior component (walls, roof, windows, doors). Generate your 2D drawings from that model rather than drawing them separately.

Phase 3 — mid-project review. Partway through, stop and check feasibility: does the plan still meet the square footage limit, does the layout still work for your target user, and is anything in the model going to be impossible to build? Record what you changed as a result.

Phase 4 — documentation and reflection. Keep your Daily Journal Log running the whole way: tasks completed, problems hit in Fusion 360, and how you solved them.

What you hand in

  • The ADU definition: type, exact square footage, target user.
  • Your conceptual floor plan sketches, and a short justification of the one you chose against the three criteria.
  • A complete Fusion 360 model.
  • A floor plan drawing: dimensioned, with the kitchen, bath and separate entrance clearly labelled.
  • An elevation view of one exterior side.
  • The mid-project feasibility check.
  • The completed Daily Journal Log.

Watch out for

The 900 sq ft ceiling is a hard constraint, not a target. Check your floor area before you start detailing, not after.

The Mid-Project Design Review and Feasibility Check is part of this project, not a separate assignment. Partway through, stop and check the square footage, the layout, and whether anything in the model would be impossible to build.

Simple Machines to Functional Mechanisms

Wk 4 Project

A handheld mechanism that comes off the printer already moving.

You move from digital designer to mechanical engineer here. The real test is the physical gap: the tolerance that lets printed plastic parts move against each other instead of fusing solid.

Part 1 — the digital foundation. Model and animate two different simple machines of your choice, each with working joints (revolute, slider, and so on).

Part 2 — the mechanism. Design one mechanical fidget or mechanism sandbox: something you hold and interact with, using at least two simple machines together. A coin vending fidget or a rack-and-pinion slider are the kind of thing.

What you hand in

  • Three renders — one per simple machine, one for the mechanism
  • Animation exports showing all three in motion
  • A motion link, so moving the input moves the outputs
  • A printed prototype that moves freely without being forced
HideRead the full brief

This project moves you from digital designer to mechanical engineer. You are not drawing shapes; you are designing a machine that has to work in the real world. The test is the physical gap: the tolerance that lets printed plastic parts move against each other instead of fusing solid.

What to do

Part 1 — the digital foundation. Model and animate two different simple machines of your choice: lever, wheel and axle, pulley, inclined plane, wedge, screw. Each must have functional joints — revolute, slider, and so on.

Part 2 — the compound prototype. Research and design one mechanical fidget or mechanism sandbox: a small handheld device using at least two simple machines working together, that you can interact with by turning a crank, pushing a lever or sliding a button. A coin vending fidget or a rack-and-pinion slider is the kind of thing.

  • Leave a tolerance gap between all moving parts. 0.3 mm is the suggested figure.
  • Use a Motion Link so that moving the input part automatically moves the outputs.
  • Print it, and make sure it moves freely.

What you hand in

  • Three high-quality renders: one per simple machine, one for the mechanism.
  • Animation video exports showing all three designs in motion.
  • A functional 3D-printed version of the compound mechanism, moving freely without being forced.

Watch out for

If two parts touch each other in Fusion, they come off the printer permanently fused. Check every interface for clearance before you send the file.

City Design

Wk 3–4 Project

The whole class as one firm, designing and printing a modern city that has to fit together.

Phase 1 — the master plan. The class acts as the urban planning board. Pick a real or fictional site in Autodesk Forma, run its analysis for sun hours, wind and noise, and let that data decide where green space goes and where the high-rises go. Then zone the city and assign each student a zone.

Phase 2 — the infrastructure standard. The class agrees one connection standard so a building designed by one student fits the road designed by another: block size, the four-sided connection system, and the exact sidewalk height and road width.

Phase 3 — architecture. Each student models onto a copy of the master base tile, using the Forma data to justify the design, and builds at least one high-detail featured building.

Phase 4 — production. Simple massing prints for the background, high detail for the featured buildings, all clicked into the master layout on the classroom table.

What you hand in

  • One digital master site plan
  • One physical scale model
  • Named roles: Project Manager holds the Forma master file and the deadlines; Infrastructure Lead prints the road and park tiles everyone connects to; Zoning Leads for residential, commercial and industrial
  • A written reflection: your role, where the interface standard held and where it failed, and what you would set differently next time
HideRead the full brief

The whole class works as one firm to design, analyse and 3D print a modern city. The class produces one digital master site plan and one physical scale model. Everything has to fit together, which makes the shared standards as important as the buildings.

What to do

Phase 1 — the master plan, in Autodesk Forma. The class acts as the urban planning board.

  • Site selection — choose a real-world location, or a fictional plot.
  • Environmental analysis — use Forma's analysis tools for sun hours, wind and noise. That data decides where green space goes and where high-rises go.
  • Zoning — divide the city into residential, commercial, industrial and transit zones. Each student or pair is assigned a zone.

Phase 2 — the infrastructure standard. The class agrees one connection standard so that a building designed by one student fits the road designed by another.

  • The grid — every city block is an agreed size and shape.
  • The connection — every tile uses the same system to connect on all four sides.
  • The road curb — one agreed sidewalk height and road width, across the whole model.

Phase 3 — architecture, in Fusion 360. Once the grid is set, build your blocks.

  • Massing — use the Forma data to justify your design decisions. If a building needs a slanted roof because of the sun it gets, say so.
  • Modular design — model directly onto a copy of the master base tile.
  • The featured building — each student produces at least one building in high detail.

Phase 4 — physical production. Simple massing prints for background buildings to save print time; high-detail prints for the featured buildings. Prints are clicked into the master layout on the classroom table as they come off.

Project roles. For the class to work as one team, these roles are assigned:

  • Project Manager (1–2 students) — holds the Forma master file and keeps everyone to their deadlines.
  • Infrastructure Lead — prints the road and park tiles that connect everyone else's work.
  • Zoning Leads — one each for residential, commercial and industrial.

What you hand in

  • Your zone, modelled on the master base tile.
  • At least one high-detail featured building.
  • Your printed tiles, fitting the class standard.
  • A written reflection: what your role was, where the interface standard held and where it failed, and what you would set differently next time.

Watch out for

Modelling before the connection standard is agreed. Anything built to your own dimensions will not fit the city, and it will have to be redone.
