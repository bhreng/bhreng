Four weeks, four separate projects. Each week you pick from the equipment available in the shop and build something with it, pairing pieces of equipment where the project calls for it. Each week's project stands alone — you are not continuing the previous one.

What to do

Start with the setup task. In a document of your choice, list, discuss and weigh project ideas for each piece of equipment before week one begins.

The equipment available:

  • Engraver
  • VEX robots, drones, robot arm
  • Shaper Origin CNC, power tools, hand tools
  • Arduinos, digital components, breadboards
  • 3D printers
  • Anything else, with approval

What a project on each looks like:

  • Engraver — a product with a 2D design cut or engraved into it.
  • Shaper Origin / power tools — a functional or decorative wooden product, accurately cut and assembled.
  • VEX robots / drones / robot arm — a functioning VEX robot, or a custom tool for the robot arm that performs a unique task.
  • 3D printers — an assembled product made of multiple printed parts.
  • Arduinos / digital components — an electronic component used in a product design or redesign.

Some weeks you will need to pair equipment. Work out which pieces go together for what you have in mind — the Shaper Origin can make a wooden base for an Arduino project, for instance.

What you hand in

Every week:

  • Monday morning — the Shop Equipment Project Weekly Planner.
  • Friday — the Shop Equipment Project Reflection.
  • Every day — the Engineering Daily Journal.
  • The finished project itself, documented.

Plus, once at the start: the Potential Ideas document.

Industrial Design Challenge: The LED Desk Lamp

Wk 1 Project

A desk lamp, judged as an industrial design object rather than a mechanism.

The brief lives in Classroom.

HideRead the full brief

Design, model and 3D print a custom desk or accent lamp housing that fully integrates the provided LED puck light. The finished design has to show industrial design proficiency and complete technical documentation. This covers ergonomics, aesthetics, design for additive manufacturing, and documentation.

What to do

Part 1 — the design constraint. The primary engineering constraint is the […]. Your design must fully encapsulate the light and provide a stable base, while still letting the power cord and switch function naturally. A generic […] with an attached power cord and on/off switch. This is purely an […] and […] challenge — you are responsible for the housing, not the electronics.

Design criteria:

  • Develop a clear, justified design theme — minimalist, organic, brutalist, futuristic. The final model has to be visually compelling and actually match that theme.
  • Diffuse, direct or focus the light into a pleasing and functional illumination pattern: for reading, mood lighting, or accent.
  • The final assembly must be stable and hold the LED puck securely, with a professional method for routing, securing and exiting the power cord.
  • Optimise for 3D printing. Minimise complex supports and multi-part assembly.

What you hand in

  • A minimum of […] for your lamp housing. Each concept must include basic overall dimensions and a scale reference, plus annotations explaining the […] and […] — for example, "inspired by mid-century modern architecture, designed to cast light upwards".
  • A […] with justification for why it was chosen for CAD development.
  • A single complete […] — .step, .f3d, or native CAD format.
  • A complete set of […] for the final design, professionally formatted, with the detail and sectional views needed, clearly indicating overall dimensions (width, height, depth) and […] for integrating the LED puck light.

Watch out for

Most of the deliverables list above has words missing in Classroom itself. You cannot tell from the post how many concept sketches to produce or what file format to submit. Ask Mr. Frank before you start, not the day it is due.

Research & Analysis: LTT Screwdriver

Wk 2 Skills

Pull apart the design decisions in one deliberately over-engineered consumer tool.

HideRead the full brief

Analysis and redesign is one of the hardest parts of the engineering design process to master, and it is among the last things you need before higher-level independent study and your senior project. Here you dissect a single product's three-year development in full detail.

What to do

  • Watch the attached video, Why our Screwdriver took 3 YEARS (29 min). The team have already done much of the breakdown; you continue it.
  • Build a document — Google Slides or Google Docs — addressing every item below, in great detail.
  • Take on the role of engineering manager: work through several of their tasks and evaluate them.
  • Find what more experienced professionals have said about the product.

Cover every step of the engineering design process:

identify the problem · research and brainstorm · develop solutions · plan a solution · create a model or prototype · test and evaluate · improve and redesign · present the solution

Cover these engineering management tasks:

design brief · work breakdown structure and timeline · team members and roles · sub-contractors and companies worked with · part list and costs

What you hand in

  • One document covering all of the above.
  • Definitions and descriptions of every word, term or phrase you did not know and that is not common knowledge.
  • A product review of the screwdriver, written by someone other than you.

Watch out for

You have one day. Budget it — the design-process breakdown is the largest part and the easiest to run out of time on.

Mars Colony Design

Wk 5 Project

A colony designed for somewhere the environment is trying to kill you.

HideRead the full brief

You are the Lead Engineering Council for the first permanent human city on Mars. This is past survival and into civilisation: you are responsible for the lives of 1,000 residents.

What to do

Part 1 — claim a sector. Take one of the seven critical sectors and design it, balancing its resources to support 1,000 people.

  • S-1 Agriculture & Hydrology — hydroponic towers and ice-melting processors. Produce 2,500,000 calories a day and manage the water reservoir.
  • S-2 Energy & Power Grid — a dual nuclear/solar array. Provide a constant 15 megawatts and manage battery storage through dust storms.
  • S-3 Atmospheric & Waste Reclamation — the city's air. CO2 scrubbers, O2 generators, and a closed loop that turns 100% of human waste back into fertiliser or water.
  • S-4 The Foundry & ISRU — the factory. Systems that mine Martian regolith to 3D print new buildings, and refine fuel for return trips to Earth.
  • S-5 Medical, Bio-Research & Genetics — the hospital. Labs for low-gravity surgery and radiation treatment, and a seed vault for Earth's plant life.
  • S-6 Logistics, Spaceport & Comms — the gateway. Landing pads for Starships, rover garages, and the deep-space laser link back to Earth.
  • S-7 Civic Center & Urban Planning — the heart. Community spaces, a 1,000-person meeting hall, a gym for bone density, and the governance centre.

Part 2 — your own residence. As senior staff you are granted a premium two-bedroom unit. Design it.

  • Footprint — 1,000 sq ft maximum.
  • Layout — at least two bedrooms, one bathroom, and a dedicated work/command station.
  • Safety — an integrated airlock.
  • Life support — show the specific utility hookup points on your blueprint where air, water and power come in from the city mainlines.

What you hand in

  • The sector facility design, with the resource numbers it has to meet.
  • The two-bedroom domicile blueprint, with the airlock and the utility hookups marked.

Watch out for

The numbers in your sector are the constraint, not decoration. A design that does not hit its calorie, megawatt or reclamation figure has not solved the problem.

Bunker House Design

Wk 5 Project

A house built for a hostile environment, on Earth this time.

HideRead the full brief

Design your own interpretation of a bunker house — a doomsday bunker, an isolation retreat, a billionaire escape, whatever version interests you. The key constraint is that it has to be genuinely self-sustaining.

What to do

Work through all five steps in order: research, brainstorm, conceptual design, detailed design, presentation.

  • Account for every utility and function of an isolated life, not just the living space.
  • Include space for food storage, growing and harvesting.
  • Account for waste.

No specific programs are required — hand drawing, 2D CAD and 3D CAD are all acceptable, and so is any form of presentation.

Designing the rooms and stopping. The self-sustaining systems are the actual assignment.

Term 2 Wk 5 → Term 4 Project

One open-ended project, chosen by you and run by you, from the end of Term 2 to the final defence.

Research, design and development of an original solution that shows what you learned in four years here. It does not fit inside a term, so it is not filed under one.

Choosing it. At the end of Term 2 you develop initial planning and concepts for three different potential ideas — or three different approaches to the same idea if you already have a strong one.

Running it. The weekly documents are not paperwork around the project; they are the evidence your final defence is built from.

What you hand in

  • Design brief, kept current
  • Daily journal, every session
  • Weekly planner on Mondays, weekly reflection on Fridays
  • Meeting notes after every instructor meeting
  • Research log — aim for one new source a day
  • Order request forms for materials and parts

An open-ended engineering project: research, design and development of an original solution that shows what you learned in four years at BHR. It starts at the end of Term 2 and runs to the final presentation.

What to do

Choosing the project (end of Term 2, into Term 3 Week 1). This is dedicated work time, not free time. Develop initial planning and concepts for three different potential project ideas. If you already have a strong idea you have discussed with the instructor, explore three different ways of approaching that same project instead. Use the time for:

  • researching existing solutions — products, technologies, approaches in your area;
  • brainstorming multiple ideas and design directions;
  • sketching, diagramming, and rough physical prototyping;
  • watching relevant videos and tutorials;
  • documenting your progress.

Revisit your past independent study documents — they hold useful material and work as a template. Create a project log recording your activities with dates and time spent.

Running the project (Term 3 onward). Every week, without exception:

  • Design brief — updated as needed.
  • Daily journal — every day.
  • Weekly planner — Mondays.
  • Weekly reflection — Fridays.
  • Meeting notes — after every meeting with the instructor.
  • Research log — track every video, article and book you draw from. Aim for at least one new source a day; in the brainstorming and research stages it should be far more than that.

Training that lands mid-project. Capstone week 5 carries Stratasys Academy online learning for the J5 series, KeyShot training, and Fusion's Texture Extrude — the resin printing and rendering skills, timed for when projects need them.

The final presentation (Term 4). Present your solution and defend the process, outcomes and critical decisions across the seven-week framework. This is not show and tell. You have to prove the value of the work:

  • The why — how you justified the problem, with evidence from patent searches and market research.
  • The how — the technical tools, EDF or ESEC focus, you used to build it.
  • The proof — your raw test data, and how your failures led to a better redesign.

Suggested nine-slide roadmap: title and introduction, with a high-quality render of the final prototype · problem justification and the credible sources that validated the need · conceptual design and the decision matrix you used to choose · technical design, with 3D CAD models and system architecture diagrams · fabrication and development, with photos of the build, assembly and coding · empirical analysis and testing, moving beyond "it works" to "here is the data" · iteration and troubleshooting, showing one specific failure and the redesign it forced · final defence and reflection · Q&A, with a final as-built view.

What you hand in

  • Three initial project concepts, before the work starts.
  • The design brief, kept current.
  • Daily journals, weekly planners, weekly reflections, meeting notes and the research log, throughout.
  • Order request forms for the materials and parts you need.
  • The functional prototype.
  • The final slide deck, uploaded to the post, with final high-resolution as-built photographs as prototype evidence.

Watch out for

The weekly documents are not paperwork around the project — they are the evidence the defence is built from. A capstone with a strong prototype and thin journals cannot answer slide 7.

Stratasys Academy online learning, KeyShot and Fusion’s Texture Extrude land in capstone week 5 — the resin printing and rendering skills, right when projects need them.

Three external courses that unlock equipment and go on your record. All three are free, and all three are listed on the training page.

What to do

VEX AIM Bot: Guided Lessons. Complete the VEX AIM Intro Course STEM Lab in your teams. It introduces the fundamental concepts of robotics and coding on the AIM system. Use the provided link and work through the lessons at a pace that is challenging but manageable for your team.

Universal Robots e-Learning. Get familiar with the URbots in the shop. Complete the e-Series Core Track modules first, then the e-Series Pro Track and the e-Series Application Track. Document your progress through each module in a Google Slide or Doc. To finish, showcase a unique program using at least one of the two URbots — you can design and make anything you need for it on the 3D printers or laser engravers.

Bambu Lab Academy — 3D printer certification. Your printer access is tied directly to your certification level:

  • Beginner on a machine — you may print on it with an instructor's assistance.
  • Intermediate — you may print with the assistance of an approved peer.
  • Advanced — you may print on it by yourself.

Machines unlock in a strict order: Level 1 A1 Mini → Level 2 X1C → Level 3 H2D. Once you complete a level on a machine you may either take the next level on that machine, or the same level on the next machine in the sequence. After Beginner on the A1 Mini, for instance, you may move to Intermediate on the A1 Mini or start Beginner on the X1C.

What you hand in

  • The VEX AIM STEM Lab, completed as a team.
  • Your Universal Robots progress document, and one unique URbot program.
  • Your Bambu Lab Academy certifications, at whatever level you have reached.

Watch out for

Every print job still has to be approved by an instructor, at every certification level. Advanced means you may run the machine alone, not that you may print whatever you like.
