Standard S8 · Mr. Frank

Industrial Design

How a thing looks, feels in the hand, and can actually be made.

You take an idea that only exists as a sketch and turn it into an object somebody would pick up and use. That means caring about the shape and the feel of it, and caring just as much about whether a machine in this building can produce it.

New to this pathway? Start here.

Nothing on this page is compulsory and there is no order you have to follow. If you want a way in, this is the one I would take.

  1. Open Combine art and engineering from Explore the Field, and ask yourself honestly whether that work appeals to you.
  2. If it does, pick one thing from Learn the Concepts — there are 3 to choose from — and actually finish it.
  3. Then go to Build Your Skills and do something with your hands. 5 options, and you do not have to take them in order.
  4. Write down what you did in your logbook, including what did not work.

Stuck, or want to talk it through? Ask Mr. Frank.

1

Explore the Field

Would this help someone decide whether to pick this pathway?

Industrial designers sit between the people who want a product and the machines that make it. The job is to make something that works, that people want to hold, and that can be manufactured without costing a fortune.

Product designerShapes the object around the person using it — size, grip, weight, how obvious it is to operate.
Manufacturing engineerWorks out how to actually produce it, at what cost, at what rate, with what waste.
Technical drafterProduces the drawings a shop builds from, using GD&T so there is no room to guess.
Research engineerDigs into materials and geometry to find out what is possible before anyone commits.
Development engineerCarries a concept through prototypes into something that can be produced.

Your Career Vision Summary asks for working conditions, education, and pay. Look up current figures yourself — the Bureau of Labor Statistics Occupational Outlook Handbook is the source to use, and it is regional.

Learn the Concepts

Is this something you study to understand how the work works?

Two ideas run through everything in this pathway: design decisions have to be justified by physics, and geometry has to be designed for the process that will produce it.

Engineering accounting
From Holtzapple & Reece, Foundations of Engineering. Treats mass, energy and momentum like a ledger: input minus output plus generation minus consumption equals accumulation. It is how you check that a design is physically possible before you build it.
Design for manufacturing
Additive geometry for 3D printing — overhangs, wall thickness, orientation. Subtractive geometry for laser and CNC — tool access, kerf, fixturing. The same part is drawn differently depending on how it will be made.
User-centered design
Ergonomics and human factors. Form follows function, but function includes "a person can figure this out without instructions."
Visual communication
Drafting standards that let a drawing say exactly one thing. Wallach, Modern Drafting, for descriptive geometry and ASME conventions; Madsen, Engineering Drawing and Design, for visualization and CAD practice.

Build Your Skills

Is this a procedure you practice until you can do it?

Credential of valueAutodesk Certified User — Fusion 360Base requirement for every pathway: OSHA 10 — Construction

Fusion 360 is the core tool here. You need both modeling styles, not just one.

  • Parametric modeling — history-driven, so changing one dimension updates everything downstream.
  • Direct modeling — for fast iteration when you are still deciding what the thing is.
  • Rendering, animation and exploded views, so someone who has never seen the part understands it.
  • GD&T on drawings — tolerances that say what actually matters on the part.
  • 3D scanners for reverse engineering an existing object.
  • 3D printers — additive protocols, material choice, print orientation.
  • Laser engravers and CNC — subtractive setup and safety.

Find a Project

Is this something you could go build?

Start from one of these or bring your own. Every one of them runs the same loop: identify and research, sketch and mass out, model and prototype, test and redesign.

Desk organizer

Constrained, fast, and unforgiving about ergonomics. Everyone thinks it is easy until they hold the first print.

Kids toy

Adds safety constraints, durability and a user who cannot read instructions.

Reverse-engineer and improve

Scan or measure an existing product, rebuild it in CAD, then change one thing and prove the change is better.

Before-and-after case study

Take something badly designed and redesign it. The deliverable is the visual story — renders and photographs that make the improvement obvious without a paragraph of explanation.
