BHR Engineering Technology · Student Guide 03

Your Engineering Logbook

You fill one in every shop day. It is the record of what you did, what did not go to plan, and what you decided — and it is the only proof that the work was yours.

Why we keep one

Every engineer who has ever had to defend a design has done it out of a logbook.

A finished project shows what worked. It does not show the three approaches you abandoned, the measurement that came back wrong twice, or the reason you switched materials in week four. Those decisions are the engineering. The logbook is where they live.

It is also how the state expects the work to be documented. Standard S3-e requires you to maintain an engineering journal for projects to document design solutions. This is ours.

Why the rules are strict

In 1943 the U.S. Supreme Court took the patent for radio away from Marconi and gave priority to Nikola Tesla. Tesla had lost the commercial race by years. What he had was dated, witnessed notebooks showing he had described the system first.

Nobody is going to sue you over a laser-cut bracket. But the habit that decided that case — write it down when it happens, date it, do not tidy it afterward — is the habit we are teaching, and it is a real one that working engineers are held to.

The five status codes

Each interval gets a code from the drop-down. Pick the one that describes what you actually spent the time on, not the one you wish you had.

PIProblem Identification Defining the problem, researching, gathering constraints and criteria.
DDDetailed Design CAD, modeling, drawings, schematics, calculations — designing the thing on screen or paper.
FABFabrication Building it. Printing, cutting, wiring, assembling, coding it up.
TETesting & Evaluation Running it, measuring it, finding out whether it does what you claimed.
IRImprove & Redesign Acting on what testing told you. Changing the design and saying why.

Five codes cover the eight stages of the design process on purpose. At the start of an interval you should not have to decide whether you are technically in "Develop Solutions" or "Plan a Solution" — pick the code that fits and get to work. The eight stages are what we use when we talk about the process itself.

One day, three intervals

Fill each one during or right after the interval it covers — not all three at 2:15.

Interval 1Prior to break
  • Work highlights and observations. Tasks completed, who you consulted, reviews, anything unexpected.
  • Roadblocks. The specific failure, and what you plan to do about it.
  • Visual evidence. A render, a diagram, a photo — captioned and described.
Interval 2Break to lunch
  • Same three fields. A new status code if the work moved on.
  • If nothing changed from Interval 1, say what you were stuck on rather than repeating yourself.
Interval 3Lunch to end of day, plus wrap-up
  • Challenges and troubleshooting. Pull together the day's failures and the workarounds you proposed.
  • Daily synthesis. A technical summary — decisions you made, and whether you are on track.
  • Next class's agenda. What you will start on when you walk in. Your future self is the reader.

The header — project engineer, date, active projects, tools and software used — gets filled in before Interval 1.

The rules

One set, for everyone, in every pathway. These replace anything different you may have read in a pathway hub guide.

  1. Write it the day it happens. An entry reconstructed from memory a week later is not a record, and it reads like one.
  2. Every day gets an entry, including bad ones. "Printer jammed twice, lost the period" is a legitimate entry. A missing day is not.
  3. Never delete. When something changes, strike the line through and write the revision underneath. The earlier thinking and the moment you revised it are both worth keeping — that pair is the evidence you analysed something rather than guessed.
  4. Record the failures in more detail than the successes. Anyone can tell what worked by looking at the finished part. Only your logbook can say what didn't.
  5. Be specific enough to be repeated. Not "adjusted settings" — which setting, from what to what, and what changed as a result.
  6. Numbers carry units. 4 mm, 0.2 in, 12 V, 240 °C. A number without a unit is not data.
  7. Every image gets a caption. Say what it shows and why you took it. An uncaptioned screenshot proves nothing.
  8. Name who helped. If a classmate solved it, a teacher pointed you somewhere, or you found it in a video, write down who or what. Credit is part of the record.
  9. Write for someone who was not there. The test is whether a student from another shop could pick up your project on Monday and know where you left it.
  10. It is yours. Your logbook covers your work. Team projects still get individual entries — what you did.

What a real entry looks like

Same interval, same student, same problem. One of these is a record.

Not an entry
INTERVAL 2 — [DD] • Worked on CAD • Had some issues • Fixed it

Nothing here can be checked, repeated, or picked up by anyone else. In six weeks it will not remind even you of what happened.

An entry
INTERVAL 2 — [DD] • Rebuilt the bracket in Fusion 360 as a single body; the two-body version kept failing the fillet at the base. • Fillet radius 3 mm errored. Dropped to 2 mm and it solved. Wall is only 2.5 mm there, so 3 mm had nowhere to go. • Asked Mr. Frank re: whether to thicken the wall instead — leaving it at 2 mm for now, revisit after the load test. ROADBLOCK: Won't know if 2 mm fillet is strong enough until Thursday's test.

Specific, with units, names the decision and the fact that it is provisional. Thursday's entry has something to answer.

How it is graded

The logbook counts twice, which is why skipping it is expensive.

WhereWhat is being looked at
Weekly gradePreparation asks whether your written instrument is present. An open, current logbook is what that means in this shop.
ClassworkThe entries themselves — completeness, specificity, and whether the day's decisions are actually recorded.
ProjectsWhen a project is reviewed, the logbook is the evidence of process. A strong final product with no record behind it is a weaker grade than it looks.

Where marks get lost

Not because the work was bad — because the record was.

Filling all three intervals at the end of the dayIt shows. Detail collapses, and the roadblock you actually solved at 10am is gone by 2pm.
Copying Interval 1 into Intervals 2 and 3Three identical entries say you stopped paying attention, not that nothing happened.
Only recording the version that workedThe abandoned attempts are the part that demonstrates engineering judgment.
Screenshots with no captionsAn image nobody explained is decoration. Say what it shows and why it is there.
Backfilling a missed dayWrite the day you missed it and why instead. An honest gap beats invented detail, and we can tell the difference.
