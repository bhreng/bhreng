How the year is shaped

Shop one week, academics the next. Everything else follows from that.

Your shop week
All five daysWhen it is your shop week you are here the whole day, every day. The following week you are in academics and not in the shop at all.
A term
About five shop weeksRoughly ten weeks on the school calendar, which alternates down to about five weeks — twenty-five full days — actually in the shop.
The school year
180 daysFour terms. About half of those days are shop days.

Why this matters more than it sounds

A week away is a long time. Whatever you were in the middle of is two weeks older by the time you next touch it, and you will not remember the detail — nobody does. This is the entire reason the logbook exists, and why the last thing you do on a Friday is write down where you got to and what comes next. Your first hour back is either a fast restart or a slow one, and your notes decide which.

How you're graded

Four categories. Notice what they add up to.

The thing most people miss

Half your grade — 50% — is how you work, not what you make. Weekly Grade and Employability together outweigh your projects. You can build something impressive and still not do well here, and you can have a project come out nothing like you planned and still have a strong term. That's deliberate: it's how you'd be judged in an actual shop.

Worth noticing

Good to Fair costs you 14 points. Fair to Unsatisfactory costs another 26. The scale isn't evenly spaced — the bottom drops away fast. "Fair" is not a comfortable place to sit.

Classroom rules and privileges

In order for us to live well with each other, we need some boundaries. Rules are not to restrict us; rules are given to us to fully enjoy the privileges of education, each other, and the equipment we have been given.

  1. Cell phones during break and lunch only. Headphones are allowed occasionally while working on individual projects.
  2. Appropriate uniform, attire, and PPE are required and are part of your weekly grade. Maintain a professional workspace and use professional language and behavior.
  3. No eating or drinking in shop common areas.
  4. Respect your teacher and classmates — which includes their property, their space, and their creativity.
  5. The Engineering Lab and its equipment are used with permission. No writing, drawing or cutting on tables. Do not intentionally misuse equipment or property.
  6. Return things to their proper places after use. Clean up after yourself and others. Follow the end-of-day cleanup routine. This is all of our workspace, so keep it clean.
  7. Stay in your assigned area. Do not leave the shop without verbal permission or an e-hall pass.
  8. Computers are for educational purposes. When your work is done, ask a teacher for more, help a classmate, or help the department.
  9. Time. Be on time for class and for deadlines. Break and end-of-day preparation don't start until the instructor grants them.
  10. Communication is key. If you have questions or concerns, it is your responsibility to ask before the assignment is due, so what you hand in is grade-level work.
  11. Always work to your potential. Prepare to be challenged.
Bonus rule: use common sense.

Some of these are privileges, and privileges are not guaranteed. Follow the rules and we keep them — here and in the shop.

Certifications and co-op

The certifications you can earn here are Autodesk — Certified User in Inventor, Fusion 360 and Revit Architecture — on top of your OSHA 10 in Grade 10. Which ones you go for depends on the work you're doing and when you're ready.

Cooperative education is available to juniors from Term 3 and to seniors from Term 1 — working in a real engineering setting for part of your schedule.

Getting help

Mr. Frank helps you make it real — designing it, drawing it, and building it. Come to him for CAD, 3D printing and laser cutting, structures, drawings, and prototypes that didn't come out right.

Mr. Dryer helps you make it work — circuits, code, and proving it performs. Come to him for wiring, debugging, sensors and motors, and showing with data that your thing does what you claim.

The official course descriptions These are the descriptions from the Engineering Technology Program Scope and Sequence, the school's current program of study. Formal wording, kept here for reference — the rest of this page is the day-to-day.

Program Overview

The Chapter 74 Engineering Technology Career Technical Education (CTE) Program prepares students with the academic, technical, and employability skills required for entry-level employment and postsecondary education in the engineering field. Aligned with the Massachusetts Chapter 74 Engineering Technology Frameworks, the program emphasizes safety, professionalism, the engineering design process, technical competence, and industry-relevant problem solving.

Students develop foundational knowledge in engineering design, computer-aided design (CAD), fabrication and prototyping, electrical and digital systems, mechanical principles, automation and robotics, and civil/architectural design. Instruction integrates classroom learning with hands-on laboratory practice in a modern engineering shop through a dual-specialization instructional model: Engineering Design & Fabrication (EDF), emphasizing the transformation of ideas into tangible forms, and Engineering Systems & Emerging Concepts (ESEC), emphasizing the intelligence, functionality, and control that drive modern engineered systems.

Assessment is competency-based and includes skill demonstrations, practical exams, written assessments, design projects, engineering logbooks, and portfolio development. By program completion, students are prepared for industry-recognized credentials, cooperative education experiences, and continued education in engineering, engineering technology, or related technical fields.

Engineering Technology I — Grade 9

Students in grade nine are introduced to foundational concepts and skills related to engineering technology. Instruction focuses on the Engineering Design Process (EDP), computer-aided design (CAD) and parametric modeling, technical measurement, basic electrical circuits and digital logic, technical graphics and documentation, workplace safety, and professional behaviors within an engineering environment. Learning experiences include classroom-based instruction and laboratory activities designed to introduce shop tools, fabrication equipment, 3D printing, and the maintenance of a professional engineering journal.

Engineering Technology II — Grade 10

Engineering Technology II builds on foundational knowledge and skills acquired in the introductory year, emphasizing technical mastery, professional documentation, and safety certification. Students deepen their understanding of electrical and analog systems, mechanical principles and simulation, quality assurance, and civil/architectural foundations (BIM/Revit) while practicing professional drafting skills, including Geometric Dimensioning & Tolerancing (GD&T). The course integrates OSHA 10 safety training — a mandatory credential at this grade level — professional ethics, and empirical analysis to prepare students for the specialized work of the junior year.

Engineering Technology III — Grade 11

Engineering Technology III is an advanced course designed for 11th-grade students to refine and apply their design, fabrication, and systems skills in industry-relevant applications. Building on prior coursework, students expand their expertise in advanced manufacturing and Design for Manufacturing (DFM) theory, control systems and mechatronics, algorithmic programming, and digital logic design. Emphasis is placed on professional project management using tools such as Gantt charts, with hands-on practice in modern technologies including CNC machining, 3D printing, programmable logic devices, and robotics. The year culminates in a 15-day Junior Capstone in architectural engineering requiring conceptual design, structural analysis, and evaluation of original concepts. Students prepare for Autodesk Certified User (ACU) examinations. Grade 11 Engineering Technology students are eligible for Cooperative Education beginning Term 3.

Engineering Technology IV / Co-op — Grade 12

Engineering Technology IV is the capstone course for 12th-grade students, designed to prepare them for professional engineering practice and postsecondary study. Students refine advanced skills in robotics and automation, computer-integrated manufacturing (CIM), quality assurance, and systems testing while upholding the highest standards of safety and professionalism. The course emphasizes leadership, employability, entrepreneurship, and continued preparation for Autodesk Certified User (ACU) examinations. The year culminates in a 35-day, self-directed Senior Capstone requiring research, design, prototyping, testing, and a formal defense, supported by a comprehensive portfolio. Grade 12 Engineering Technology students are eligible for Cooperative Education beginning Term 1.
